=== earth-quakes
idea: The ground under your feet never stops moving. Find out how we measure it, and how a ten-year-old on a beach used what she had learned.
game: P wave = The push-pull wave that arrives first | S wave = The sideways wave that does more damage | Chile 1960 = The strongest earthquake ever measured | Tsunami in deep water = Moves over 500 mph, barely lifts the sea | Tilly Smith = The ten-year-old who cleared her beach | Kola borehole = The deepest hole ever drilled

--- 🌍
p: Stand still for a moment. It feels like you are not moving at all. But Earth's surface is cracked into giant slabs called plates, and they creep along at about the speed your fingernails grow.
fact(How we know): Nobody has watched a plate move. But GPS receivers are bolted to bedrock all over the world, and each one reports its own position. Year after year, those positions shift by a few inches.
try: Draw a line on paper 4 inches long. That is about as far as the quickest plates travel in a whole year. The slowest move about one inch.
check: What shows us that the plates are moving?
- Cameras that film the ground all year
- Drills that measure how hot the rock is
+ GPS receivers bolted to bedrock
hint: Look at the How we know box. It names what is bolted to the rock.
explain: Each receiver reports its own position, and those positions keep shifting by a few inches a year.

--- 📈
p: Where plates grind past each other, the ground sometimes jerks. That is an earthquake. Most are far too small to feel, and thousands are recorded every single day.
term: magnitude = The number that tells you how big an earthquake is. You may hear it called the Richter scale, but today's numbers come from a newer scale that agrees with it in the middle.
fact(Big ones are rare): Across the years 2000 to 2021, the world averaged about 135 quakes of magnitude 6 a year, around 13 of magnitude 7, and roughly one of magnitude 8 or stronger.
check: About how many magnitude 7 earthquakes happen in a typical year?
- About 1
+ About 13
- About 135
hint: Each step up gets rarer. Magnitude 6 is about 135 a year, and magnitude 8 is about one.
explain: Around 13 a year. Magnitude 6 is about 135, and 8 or stronger is about one.

--- 🔟
p: Here is the surprise about magnitude. The numbers do not climb like a ruler.
fact: Every whole step up the scale means the shaking is ten times bigger, and about 32 times as much energy comes out.
p: So a magnitude 7 is not a little worse than a 6. It would take roughly 32 sixes to add up to one seven.
check: About how many magnitude 6 earthquakes would it take to match the energy of one magnitude 7?
- About 10
+ About 32
- About 100
hint: One whole step up is about 32 times the energy.
explain: About 32. That is why a single step up the scale is such a big jump.

--- 🌊
p: So how big can an earthquake get? The strongest ever measured struck southern Chile, near Valdivia, on 22 May 1960.
fact(The record): Magnitude 9.5. The shaking lasted about ten minutes, and hundreds of miles of coastline tore at once.
p: And it was not over. The tsunami it launched was still killing people in Hawaii and Japan a day later.

--- ✈️
p: A tsunami is a wave, but nothing like the ones you jump in at the beach.
fact: In deep water it crosses the ocean at over 500 mph, about the speed of an airliner, while lifting the surface only a few feet.
p: A ship sitting right on top of one feels nothing unusual. The wave only piles up into something terrible when it reaches shallow water. Buoys in the deep ocean feel it slip underneath and radio a warning to the coast.
check: A ship is floating right on top of a tsunami, far out at sea. What does the crew notice?
- A wall of water towering over the deck
+ Almost nothing
- The sea draining away
hint: In deep water the wave lifts the surface only a few feet.
explain: Almost nothing. The wave only piles up when it reaches shallow water.

--- 🏖️
p: In 2004, a ten-year-old girl named Tilly Smith was standing on Mai Khao Beach in Thailand. The water looked wrong to her.
fact(The warning signs): A tsunami announces itself. Sometimes the sea drains away first and leaves fish stranded. Sometimes the water comes in and in and never goes out, frothing oddly.
p: Tilly knew those signs from a geography lesson two weeks earlier. She told her parents. Her father told a hotel security guard. The beach was cleared before the wave arrived, and Mai Khao was one of very few beaches on Phuket where nobody was killed.
check: How did Tilly know that something was wrong with the water?
- She had felt the ground shake beneath her feet
+ She had learned the signs in a geography lesson
- A lifeguard on the beach warned her to run away
hint: She had learned about it two weeks before.
explain: A geography lesson, two weeks earlier. What she had learned protected a whole beach.

--- 📈
p: Every earthquake sends out two kinds of wave from the same spot at the same moment. One races ahead and one lags behind. The gap between them tells you how far away the quake was.
term: P wave = The push-pull wave. It is the fastest through rock, so it always arrives first.
term: S wave = The sideways wave. It lags behind the P wave, and it does more damage.
check: What can you work out from the gap between the P wave and the S wave?
+ How far away the earthquake was
- How many buildings it knocked down
- How long the shaking lasted
hint: One wave is faster than the other, so the gap grows with the distance they travel.
explain: The farther away the quake, the longer the wait between the two waves.

--- ⛏️
p: Now a puzzle. How far down have people actually been? The deepest hole ever drilled is the Kola borehole in Russia, finished in 1989 at 40,230 feet (12,262 m). That is 7.6 miles straight down.
fact: The rock at the bottom was around 356 degrees F (180 C), far hotter than planned, and the drilling stopped. Kola never came close to leaving the crust.
p: Nobody has ever been below the crust, and nobody has seen the core. So what do we know about it?

--- 🔔
fact(Worked out, not dug up): Sideways S waves cannot travel through liquid, and they vanish partway down. So the outer core is reasoned to be molten.
p: In 1936, a Danish seismologist named Inge Lehmann noticed waves arriving where a fully liquid core should have left silence. She argued there was a solid inner core inside the liquid one. No camera and no sample, just wiggles on a chart.
check: Why do we think Earth's outer core is liquid?
- A drill brought up melted rock
- A camera was lowered into the core
+ Sideways waves vanish partway down
hint: Nobody has been there. Think about what earthquake waves do.
explain: S waves cannot pass through liquid, and they vanish partway down. It is worked out, not seen.

--- 🏗️
p: People do not just measure earthquakes. They build to survive them.
fact: Some buildings do not sit on the ground at all. They sit on thick pads of rubber layered with steel and lead. When the ground jerks sideways, the pads squash and bend sideways, and the building above lags behind, moving far less than the dirt under it.
p: Hospitals, museums and emergency centers are built this way in Japan, New Zealand and California.

--- 📜
p: So where did the ground come from in the first place?
fact(The written account says): On day three in Genesis, the waters under the sky are gathered into one place, and dry land appears. Water first, and then land standing out of it, which the account calls Earth.

=== earth-rocks
idea: Almost half of every rock is oxygen, and salt is a mineral. Look closer at the ground, and find out why a ruby and a sapphire are the same stone.
game: Obsidian = Glass that a volcano made | Halite = Breaks into tiny cubes | Corundum = The mineral behind ruby and sapphire | Naica = The Mexican mine of giant crystals | Pumice = Full of bubbles, so it floats | Mica = Black flecks that peel in shiny sheets

--- 🪨
p: Pick up a rock and hold it. Here is a secret about what is inside. Nearly half of it, by weight, is oxygen, the same element you are breathing right now, locked into minerals instead of floating loose.
fact(By weight): Oxygen makes up 46 percent of Earth's crust. Silicon is another 28 percent, and aluminum is about 8. Most rock is those three joined together, with some iron mixed in.
check: Which element makes up the biggest share of Earth's crust?
- Iron
- Silicon
+ Oxygen
hint: Look at the first number on the page. It is the same element you breathe.
explain: Oxygen, at 46 percent. Together with silicon and aluminum, it makes up more than four fifths of everything you walk on.

--- 🔍
p: Look at a polished granite countertop. All those speckles look like one speckled stone. They are not.
fact: Each speck is a separate mineral, grown together with its neighbors. Glassy gray grains are quartz. Blocky pink or white ones are feldspar. The black flecks are mica, and they peel off in shiny sheets.
try: Put your eye close to a granite counter, step or gravestone. You can usually pick out three or four different kinds of grain without a magnifier.
check: What are the speckles you see in granite?
+ Separate minerals grown together
- Paint that was added to the stone
- Tiny pieces of glass
hint: Look at the page again. Quartz, feldspar and mica are all in there.
explain: Granite is a mixture. You are looking at several minerals, each with its own look.

--- 🧂
p: Here is a mineral you have probably already eaten today.
term: halite = The proper name for table salt. It is a rock that people eat every day.
fact: Halite always breaks along the same three directions, so its grains come apart into tiny cubes.
try: Tip a little salt onto dark paper and look closely. The cubes are right there.

--- ✋
p: How do you tell one mineral from another? You find out what scratches what.
fact(The hardness scale): A mineral's hardness is really a list of what it can scratch. Talc, number 1, gives way to a fingernail. Quartz, number 7, cuts glass. Diamond sits at 10, and the only thing that reliably scratches a diamond is another diamond.
try: Your fingernail is about 2.5 on the scale, and a copper coin is about 3. A rock your nail marks is softer than calcite. A rock that shrugs off a steel nail is harder than about 5.5.
check: A rock shrugs off a steel nail. What do you know about it?
- It is softer than your fingernail
+ It is harder than about 5.5
- It must be a diamond
hint: Look at the try it box. The steel nail sits at about 5.5.
explain: It is harder than about 5.5. That is all this test tells you. It does not say which mineral it is.

--- 📏
p: The scale is named for Friedrich Mohs, a German mineral expert. In 1812 he lined up ten minerals in the order of which scratched which. He never measured a force or built a machine. He just made a ranking, and it is still in every geology kit.
fact(Not a ruler): The scale looks like a ruler, but it is not one. Corundum, a very hard mineral, is 9 and diamond is 10, one step apart. Yet measured properly with a hard point, diamond resists about four times better than corundum.
check: Corundum is 9 and diamond is 10. How much harder is diamond, really?
- Exactly one step harder
- Just a tiny bit harder
+ About four times harder
hint: The numbers are only an order of finish, not a measurement.
explain: About four times better at resisting a hard point. The gaps between the ten steps are all different sizes.

--- 💎
p: Now a magic trick. Ruby and sapphire are the same mineral.
term: corundum = The mineral that both rubies and sapphires are. It is aluminum and oxygen.
fact: A trace of chromium turns it red, and it gets called ruby. Traces of iron and titanium turn it blue, and it gets called sapphire. Same hardness, same recipe, a different pinch of something extra, and a wildly different price.

--- 🌋
p: Volcanoes make rocks that do surprising things. Start with glass.
fact: Obsidian is glass that a volcano made. The lava cooled so fast that its minerals never got to line up into crystals. With no grain to follow, a flake of obsidian can come to an edge a few nanometers across. A nanometer is a billionth of a meter, and that edge is far thinner than a steel razor's. People chipped it into knives and arrowheads.
check: Why can obsidian get such a sharp edge?
- It is the hardest mineral there is
+ It has no crystal grain to follow
- It is heated until it is soft
hint: Look at the second sentence. It says what the minerals never got to do.
explain: The minerals never lined up into crystals, so it breaks to an edge only a few nanometers across.

--- 🫧
p: And here is a rock that floats.
fact: Pumice is rock full of frozen gas bubbles. After some eruptions, whole rafts of it drift across the open sea, and ships and satellites track them for months. In the end the pumice waterlogs and sinks.
try: Drop a piece in a glass of water and it bobs. Leave it long enough, and water works into the holes and it goes under.

--- 🕳️
p: Now the biggest crystals anyone has ever found.
fact(Naica, Mexico): Miners at Naica in Mexico broke into a chamber packed with clear gypsum beams. The largest measure about 37 feet (11 m) long and 13 feet (4 m) thick, and weigh roughly 55 tons. They are single crystals.
p: The chamber sits near 136 degrees F (58 C), and the air is almost completely saturated with water. Even in cooled suits, people could only stay for a short time, well under an hour. In October 2015 the mine's pumps were switched off, and the water came back and covered the crystals again.
check: How long could people stay inside the Naica crystal chamber, even in cooled suits?
- A whole working day
- Several hours
+ Well under an hour
hint: The chamber sits near 136 degrees F, and the air is full of water.
explain: Well under an hour at a time. It was far too hot and wet to stay longer.

--- 📜
p: Genesis names minerals early. Describing the land of Havilah in chapter 2, it lists gold, bdellium and onyx stone.
fact: Onyx is banded quartz, number 7 on the hardness scale, and people still cut and polish it.
p: So the next time you pick up a rock, look closer. Grains, cubes, a pinch of chromium. There is a lot to read in one small stone.

=== earth-volcanoes
idea: Two words mean almost the same thing, and mixing them up is a trap. Find out how anyone knows what is hiding under a volcano when nobody has ever looked.
game: Magma chamber = Worked out from earthquake waves | Plinian eruption = Named after Pliny the Younger's description | Ol Doinyo Lengai = Erupts coolest lava on Earth | Tambora = Caused the year without a summer | USGS = Keeps watch on volcanoes in the United States

--- 🌋
p: Two words that mean almost the same thing, and mixing them up is a trap: magma and lava.
term: magma = The melted rock underground.
term: lava = The melted rock once it comes out of the volcano.
check: What is melted rock called once it comes out of the vent?
- Magma
- Rock
+ Lava
hint: It changes its name when it leaves the ground.
explain: Once the melted rock is out, it is called lava.

--- 🏔️
p: Here’s a surprise: measured from its base on the sea floor, Mauna Kea in Hawaii is taller than Mount Everest.
fact: Mauna Kea rises about 30,610 feet (9,330 m) from the sea floor. Everest stands 29,032 feet (8,849 m) above sea level.
p: But most of Mauna Kea is underwater. Only 13,803 feet (4,207 m) of it sticks out above the water.
check: Measured from the sea floor, which mountain is taller?
- Mount Everest
+ Mauna Kea
- They are the same height
hint: Look at the first number on the page.
explain: Mauna Kea rises about 30,610 feet from the sea floor, making it taller than Everest.

--- 🎬
p: Films often get it wrong. Most lava flows creep along slower than you walk, and people stand next to them taking photographs.
fact: The real killer is the pyroclastic flow: a ground-hugging cloud of hot ash and gas that comes down the slope faster than 50 mph (80 km/h).
p: That is what reached Pompeii and Herculaneum. Volcanologists worry less about where the lava is going and more about whether the eruption column is about to collapse.
check: Which volcano hazard is the real killer?
+ The pyroclastic flow
- The glowing lava river
- The small earthquakes before the eruption
hint: Look at the fact box for a description of the killer.
explain: The pyroclastic flow moves faster than 50 mph and can devastate everything in its path.

--- 🔥
p: How hot is lava? At Kilauea in Hawaii, it comes out around 2,140 degrees F (1,170 C).
fact: That temperature is not guessed at. Geologists walk up to the flow and push a metal probe called a thermocouple straight into it.
p: Some flows cannot be safely reached. Then they point calibrated thermal cameras at those instead. The camera readings are checked against the ones taken with the probe.

--- ⚫
p: Most lava glows red hot, but one volcano spits out something much cooler.
fact: Ol Doinyo Lengai in Tanzania erupts the coolest lava on Earth, about 930 to 1,110 degrees F (500 to 600 C). It is cool enough that it does not glow in daylight.
p: This lava pours out looking like runny black oil. When rain hits it, the hardened flows turn chalk white.
p: Most lava is made of the same minerals as ordinary rock. Ol Doinyo Lengai's lava is made of sodium and potassium salts instead. That is why it is so runny and cool, and why water turns it white.

--- 📜
p: The eruption that buried Pompeii in AD 79 had an eyewitness. Pliny the Younger watched it from across the bay.
term: Plinian = A type of eruption with a tall column punching straight up into the sky, named after Pliny the Younger's description.
fact: Pliny the Younger wrote two letters to historian Tacitus describing a cloud shaped like an umbrella pine. It had a tall trunk with branches spreading at the top.
check: What did Pliny say the cloud looked like?
- A tall wall of water
- A volcanic cone
+ An umbrella pine
hint: His description mentions a tall trunk with branches spreading at the top.
explain: Pliny described the cloud as looking like an umbrella pine.

--- 💥
p: Krakatoa exploded on the morning of 27 August 1883.
fact: People 3,000 miles away on Rodrigues Island heard a distant roar that sounded like heavy guns. Perth, Australia, heard it too, and it was 1,930 miles off.
p: Barographs recorded something strange happening. These instruments scratch a line of air pressure onto paper. The lines showed the pressure wave passing seven times over five days.
p: It went out to the far side of the world and back, out and back, until it faded. One island's blast had been felt around the whole planet, more than once.

--- 🥶
p: Tambora in Indonesia erupted in April 1815 and blew about 4,200 feet off its own summit.
fact: The next year, farmers in New England wrote down frost in every single month and a snowstorm in June. They called 1816 the year without a summer, and also Eighteen Hundred and Froze to Death.
check: What did people call 1816?
- The year of the great flood
+ The year without a summer
- The year of endless sunshine
hint: People wrote down frost in every month that year.
explain: They called it the year without a summer because there was frost in every single month and even snow in June.

--- 📊
p: How many volcanoes are out there? About 1,350 on land could erupt.
fact: Of those, about 500 have actually erupted within written history. On a typical day, roughly 20 are erupting somewhere in the world.
p: About 160 of those volcanoes sit in the United States and its territories. That is why the USGS keeps watch on them with volcano observatories in Hawaii, Alaska, California, Washington, and Yellowstone.

--- 🔍
p: Here is the puzzle: nobody has ever looked into a magma chamber.
fact(Worked out, not seen): Their size and depth are worked out from how earthquake waves slow down when passing through melted rock and from the ground swelling by a few inches before an eruption.
check: How do we know about magma chambers?
+ Worked out from earthquake waves slowing and swelling ground
- By drilling down and sending a camera into each chamber
- By melting a piece of cooled lava and timing how it hardens
hint: Look at the fact box. It tells you what scientists measure.
explain: Earthquake waves slow down in melted rock, and the ground swells before an eruption. These clues help scientists work out the details.

--- 📜
p: Volcanoes throw things up out of the ground. So where does the written account put the water of the Flood?
fact(The written account says): In Genesis 7, on the same day the fountains of the great deep were broken up and the windows of heaven were opened. The water out of the ground is named first.

=== earth-weather
idea: Lightning heats the air five times hotter than the sun's surface. Find out how to measure the distance of a lightning strike by counting.
game: Lightning bolt = Heats air five times hotter than the sun | Wilson Bentley = First to photograph a snow crystal | Vostok station = Coldest temperature recorded | Bridge Creek, Oklahoma = Fastest wind in a tornado measured | cumulus cloud = Holds about a million pounds of water

--- ⚡
p: A storm is coming. Imagine you see a lightning bolt flash across the sky.
fact: A lightning bolt heats the air in its path to about 50,000 F (27,800 C). The surface of the sun is around 10,000 F (5,500 C). So the air inside a lightning channel is roughly five times hotter than the surface of the sun.
p: But it only lasts for about a ten-thousandth of a second. Lightning is a huge current of electricity pushing through air, which resists it so much that it heats up the air around it.
check: How hot is the air in a lightning bolt compared with the sun's surface?
- About fifty times hotter
+ About five times hotter
- Only about twice as hot
hint: Look at the first fact on the page. It tells you the temperatures.
explain: The air inside a lightning channel is about five times hotter than the surface of the sun.

--- 🔊
p: So what about that boom that follows lightning? Thunder is the sound of air superheated by a lightning bolt blowing outward.
fact: Light gets to you almost instantly, but sound crawls at about one mile every five seconds. Count from the flash to the bang, divide by five, and that tells you roughly how many miles off the strike was.
try: The next time there's a storm, count one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi from the flash until the thunder arrives. Divide by five for miles.
check: You count ten seconds from the flash to the bang. How far away is the strike?
+ About two miles
- About one mile
- About five miles
hint: Look at the fact box. It tells you how to work it out.
explain: Counting ten seconds means the lightning strike was about two miles away.

--- ☁️
p: Look up at a puffy cumulus cloud. It holds around 1.1 million pounds of water, about as much as a loaded jumbo jet.
fact: The water is chopped into droplets so small they cannot fall quickly. Rising air underneath keeps shoving them back up, and the droplets are falling extremely slowly.
p: Rain starts when those tiny droplets bump into each other and merge. Once they get heavy enough, they win the battle and start to fall.

--- 🧊
p: Have you ever seen hail? The biggest one in the US fell on Vivian, South Dakota.
fact: It was 8 inches across and weighed 1.94 pounds, about the size of a soccer ball made of ice.
p: By the time it was measured, it had shrunk a little from sitting in a home freezer. So when it first landed, it was even bigger than that record.

--- 🧅
p: Cut a big hailstone open and you find layers, like an onion.
fact(Worked out): The old idea was that each layer is one trip up and down inside the storm. That is only a guess. Nobody can follow a hailstone around inside a thundercloud.
p: The layers form as the stone drifts through patches of different temperature and wetness. Clear layers mean water landed on the stone faster than it could freeze, so the water spread out and the air bubbles escaped. Cloudy white layers mean it froze on contact and trapped the bubbles.
check: Why can we not say exactly how a hailstone grew?
- Scientists have never found a hailstone that has layers
- Hailstones are made on the ground, not in the clouds
+ Nobody can follow one around inside a thundercloud
hint: Look at the Worked out box. It says nobody can follow a hailstone inside a thundercloud.
explain: Nobody can follow a hailstone around inside a thundercloud, so we cannot track its exact path and growth.

--- ❄️
p: Have you ever tried to look at a snow crystal up close? Wilson Bentley did.
fact: On 15 January 1885, he made the first photograph of a single snow crystal. He was nineteen years old and lived on a farm in Jericho, Vermont. It had taken him two winters of failures to get that one picture.
p: Bentley kept going for the rest of his life. He photographed more than 5,000 snow crystals. His book "Snow Crystals" came out in 1931 with 2,453 pictures in it.

--- ⛄
p: Snow crystals have six sides for a reason.
term: crystal = A solid made up of molecules that lock into a repeating pattern.
fact: When water freezes, its molecules lock into a repeating six-sided pattern. The snow crystal grows outward on this frame, so it ends up with six sides.
check: Why do snow crystals have six sides?
- Because the wind carves every flake into six sharp points.
+ Because ice molecules lock into a six-sided pattern.
- Because every snowflake grows from six drops of water.
hint: The fact box tells you why the molecules form a six-sided pattern.
explain: Ice molecules lock into a six-sided pattern, and the crystal grows outward from there.

--- 🔥
p: The hottest air temperature ever officially recorded is 134 F (56.7 C). It was read at Greenland Ranch in Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913.
fact(Still argued about): Some researchers say the observer might have misread the thermometer that week. But Death Valley still regularly gets past 125 F (52 C) today.

--- 🥶
p: At the other end, the coldest air temperature ever recorded was minus 128.6 F (minus 89.2 C).
fact: This record was set at the Soviet Vostok station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983. July is midwinter there.
p: If you throw a cup of boiling water into the air at that temperature, it can turn to ice crystals before it lands.
check: Where was the coldest air ever recorded?
+ Vostok station in Antarctica
- Greenland Ranch in Death Valley
- The North Pole
hint: The fact box tells you the exact location.
explain: The coldest air was measured at Vostok station in Antarctica.

--- 🌪️
p: The fastest wind ever measured in a tornado was read by a radar mounted on a truck near Bridge Creek, Oklahoma.
fact: On 3 May 1999, the radar read about 301 mph (484 km/h), give or take about 20 mph.
p: The radar was looking at wind a few hundred feet up, not at ground level. No weather instrument on the ground survives a tornado, so tornado strength is normally rated afterward from what the damage looks like.
check: How is a tornado's strength normally rated?
- By a weather instrument left standing in its path
- By how long it lasts
+ Afterward, from the damage
hint: The page says ground instruments do not survive.
explain: Since no ground instruments survive a tornado, its strength is rated afterward by looking at the damage.

--- 📜
p: On day two, the written account says the waters were divided, with some below and some above.
fact(The written account says): The water above is overhead right now. One ordinary afternoon cloud carries a million pounds of it.
p: That a cloud holds about a million pounds of water is weighed and calculated. Where the water above came from is read out of Genesis.

=== earth-caves
idea: A hole can open under a house, and a cave can hold animals that never see the sun. Find out how big caves get, and what nobody has ever watched happen.
game: Mammoth Cave = The longest cave known, 426 miles | Veryovkina = The deepest known cave on Earth | Winter Park = The sinkhole that swallowed a house | Ho Khanh = The man who found Hang Son Doong | Movile Cave = Home to animals with no eyes | Mount St. Helens = Caused a massive flood when it erupted

--- 🕳️
